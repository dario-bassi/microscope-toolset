## Abstract

In many real-world scenarios, rewards extrinsic to the agent are extremely sparse, or absent altogether. In such cases, curiosity can serve as an intrinsic reward signal to enable the agent to explore its environment and learn skills that might be useful later in its life. We formulate curiosity as the error in an agent's ability to predict the consequence of its own actions in a visual feature space learned by a self-supervised inverse dynamics model. Our formulation scales to high-dimensional continuous state spaces like images, bypasses the difficulties of directly predicting pixels, and, critically, ignores the aspects of the environment that cannot affect the agent. The proposed approach is evaluated in two environments: VizDoom and Super Mario Bros. Three broad settings are investigated: 1) sparse extrinsic reward, where curiosity allows for far fewer interactions with the environment to reach the goal; 2) exploration with no extrinsic reward, where curiosity pushes the agent to explore more efficiently; and 3) generalization to unseen scenarios (e.g. new levels of the same game) where the knowledge gained from earlier experience helps the agent explore new places much faster than starting from scratch.

## Smart microscopy principle (aspirational)

Pathak et al. is a **reinforcement-learning / video-game** paper, not a microscopy paper. It is included here as the aspirational paradigm explicitly flagged by Ward et al. 2026 (Small Methods, "Self-Driving Microscopes") §5.2 as the recipe for novelty-driven smart-microscopy acquisition. The relevant Ward passage (ref 115 in the bibliography is this paper) reads verbatim:

> "the ability to control the environment of the sample could be exploited to discover novel phenotypes. Here, curiosity-driven reinforcement learning models specifically trained to generate novel observations hold particular promise. While curiosity-driven RL currently requires – in the context of super-resolution microscopy – an infeasible amount of training data, more recent algorithms significantly reduce the size of datasets required by enabling fast training on real-world data. At the same time, the automation of the sample handling and imaging process enables a much higher rate of data collection, potentially making curiosity-driven RL a more feasible approach as both technologies mature."

This places Pathak in the same aspirational-paradigm slot as [[Papers/Liang 2023]]: the published paper itself is in another field (RL/Atari for Pathak; tabletop manipulators for Liang), but a smart-microscopy review explicitly imports its recipe as the future shape of the field.

The transfer to microscopy is conceptually direct: a microscope is an embodied agent with a perception channel (camera) and a discrete action API (set XY, set Z, set channel, set exposure, snap). Pathak's three ingredients map cleanly:

1. **Intrinsic reward = prediction error.** The agent maintains a forward model that predicts the next observation in a learned feature space given the current observation and the chosen action. The reward at each step is the prediction error of that forward model on the actually observed next frame. For microscopy this becomes "image whatever maximises prediction error on a feature-space forward model of the sample" — i.e. the microscope autonomously visits FOVs / channels / Z-planes whose appearance the agent does *not yet* know how to predict from its action history. This is the formal, quantitative version of "image whatever the network hasn't seen before" that the smart-microscopy reviews keep describing in plain English.
2. **Inverse dynamics representation, not pixel prediction.** The crucial trick is that the feature space is learned by training an *inverse* dynamics model (predict the action that connects two consecutive frames). This forces the representation to encode only what the agent's actions can affect, and to ignore everything else. The microscopy translation is sharp: a curiosity-driven microscope must be rewarded for novelty in the *controllable* part of the image (cell phenotype, expression pattern, organelle morphology) and *not* for novelty in confounders the microscope cannot influence (camera read noise, illumination flicker, focus jitter, stage-vibration speckle). Pixel-level prediction error would be dominated by the confounders; inverse-dynamics features are the principled way to gate them out.
3. **Generalisation across scenarios.** Pathak shows the curiosity policy learned on VizDoom level 1 transfers to unseen levels — the agent uses what it already knows to focus exploration on what is genuinely new. The microscopy analogue is reusable across-experiment exploration: a curiosity policy trained across many cell lines / conditions should focus its acquisition budget on phenotypes the model genuinely has not seen, rather than re-discovering "interesting" vesicles every plate.

## What microscopy use this enables

Concretely, the recipe positions a smart microscope as an **unsupervised novelty detector that picks acquisition targets** without a hand-defined event trigger. Two acquisition shapes that today's event-triggered systems cannot deliver:

- **Phenotype discovery in screens with no pre-specified rare-event class.** Existing event-driven smart microscopy ([[Papers/Mahecic 2022]], [[Papers/Alvelid 2022]], [[Papers/Stepp 2026]]) requires the operator to specify *what* counts as an event — mitosis, calcium transient, exocytosis, organelle contact. Curiosity-driven RL inverts the question: spend the high-resolution / high-dose / slow-modality budget on whatever *the agent's forward model is currently worst at predicting*. The trigger is intrinsic to the agent, not engineered into the protocol. For unbiased phenotypic screens this is the natural formalism.
- **Active sampling of structure-property landscapes.** A curiosity policy on a sample whose action space includes "move stage to (x,y)", "set illumination", "set probe field" steers the microscope toward regions where the property-from-structure model is most uncertain — i.e. the experimentally informative regions. Vatsavai et al. 2025 (arXiv:2504.20011) is the first explicit AFM realisation of this idea in materials science; the in-vivo / live-cell version remains aspirational.

The translation to `pymmcore-plus` is mechanical in shape: the action space is exactly the device-property API Pycro-Manager / pymmcore-plus already exposes ([[Papers/Pinkard 2021]], [[Papers/Edelstein 2010]]); the observation is the snapped frame; the forward model is a small CNN that predicts next-frame features; the reward is its squared error on the observed next frame. This is the Mahecic-2022 / Alvelid-2022 plumbing with a different scoring head.

## Caveats — why this is aspirational, not deployable today

Ward 2026 is candid that the recipe currently requires "an infeasible amount of training data" for super-resolution microscopy, and points to model-based RL successors (DayDreamer / DreamerV3, Wu 2022 and Hafner 2023 — refs 116/117 in Ward) as the bridge that *might* make on-instrument curiosity training feasible. Three concrete obstacles inherited from Pathak's setting:

1. **Sample-budget asymmetry.** VizDoom and Super Mario Bros provide unlimited free observations; live-cell super-resolution provides a finite photon budget per cell and a finite cell budget per experiment. A naive Pathak agent that maximises prediction error will photobleach / phototoxify the sample exploring uninformative patches before its forward model converges. Any in-vivo realisation must hard-clip dose under the curiosity policy ([[Papers/Mandal 2025]] makes the same point for LLM agents — never delegate dose to the planner).
2. **Action-affecting features are samplewide, not per-frame.** Pathak's inverse-dynamics trick ignores features the agent cannot influence. On a microscope, *biological state evolves whether or not the microscope acts* — most of the interesting variance is technically not action-affecting. The naive inverse-dynamics representation would discard exactly the signal the experiment is about. This is the deep adaptation barrier: microscopy curiosity needs a forward model conditioned on *time + action*, not action alone, and the formulation in Pathak does not provide it.
3. **Reward sparsity is a feature, not a bug.** Pathak motivates curiosity for environments where extrinsic reward is sparse. Most acquisition-control papers ([[Papers/Bouchard 2023]], [[Papers/Ye 2025]], [[Papers/Mahecic 2022]]) do have a meaningful extrinsic reward (event detected, downstream-task uncertainty, classifier score) — they sit on the *opposite* end of the reward-density axis from Pathak's intended use. The curiosity recipe is most relevant precisely where current smart microscopy is weakest: open-ended phenotypic discovery with no pre-defined event class.

The pragmatic reading: Pathak gives the field a clean, citable formalism for what "novelty-driven acquisition" should mean — prediction error in an action-conditioned feature space — and for why the obvious-but-wrong baseline (pixel-level reconstruction error) fails. Whether the formalism is on-instrument-deployable today is an open question Ward 2026 leaves to model-based-RL follow-up.
